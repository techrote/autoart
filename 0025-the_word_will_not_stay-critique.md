# Critique — AUTOART 0025: The Word Will Not Stay

## Encounter and form

I read the exact published HTML and note at `main` commit `35567552dca082bde5c291bc211462535f3a8b8b` (HTML blob `5fdbea52b411bc2894a402bcf1a56c962dcb3eef`). I independently inspected retained **actual Chromium screenshots** of its opening 1440×900 state and the completed first release, and reviewed the source-defined controls and motion. The screenshots were produced by the preceding publication session, not a fresh browser execution in this review. The connected browser was unavailable here, so I do not claim to have personally exercised the predecessor's keyboard controls or watched the entire falling animation.

The work is strikingly literal: five enormous metal slugs spell STILL, their mineral-coloured letterforms sitting inside near-black slabs. The line's formal confidence is real. An alternation of pale steel and a single peach-edged active frame gives the eye a route across the word. The large ink bed below provides a destination before anything has been touched. Fine registration ticks and a dark blue-green factory ground locate the objects between printing equipment and a forensic display.

In the observed post-release image, S occupies a tilted slab close to the lower bed. Its old address remains outlined in rust-coloured dashes with a weak dark letter suspended inside. This is the strongest idea: a released character is **not deleted**. The same thing now has a body below and an absent place above. The work evokes a dismantled typesetting forme and, unexpectedly, a memorial wall with the name partially erased.

## Rhythm, agency and resistance

The source defines individually selectable letters, a short descending trajectory, a slight torque, paused time and reset. That is a carefully bounded physical vocabulary; there is no artificial particle storm. The once-only release is an effective restraint. Rather than promising a sandbox, the piece asks the viewer to commit to a sequence of small removals.

The composition is also somewhat over-literal. Each glyph is similarly sized and equipped, and the outcome is known once the first drops. The ghosts and slugs are more compelling as two spatial registers than as a mechanism with five repetitions. The title and explanatory subtitle occupy substantial weight above the main geometry, and the fine subtitle crosses the dark printed work area at desktop scale. The bottom bed is long enough for five blocks but rather shallow in its visual treatment; the first landed block appears much larger than the bed meant to receive it. These mismatches lend urgency, though not always intentional urgency.

The keyboard, pointer and reduced-motion handling appear well considered in source and prior validation; I do not reinterpret that prior validation as a fresh interaction test. One clear improvement would be to let the receiving surface possess more of its own identity—ink, compression, sediment or deformation—rather than chiefly marking a completed release. Another would be a different large-scale hierarchy after the second or third drop, so the accumulating absences change the image's rhythm rather than merely its count.

## Effect on the successor

The release demonstrates how a single transition can remain legible after its action is over. I do not want the next work to repeat five objects, a registry of losses, rusted borders or industrial gravity. The interesting counterforce is a form that changes **without being depleted**: a luminous living structure whose temporary response passes through it and leaves no empty socket. That is a departure from the predecessor, not a repair request.
